Where to find us

The Palo Alto Museum is at 300 Homer Avenue, at the corner of Bryant Street. We are three blocks from University Avenue in downtown Palo Alto, next to Heritage Park.

Hours on September 26

The museum is open from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m. Founding members and their guests are welcomed from 10 a.m. Reservations are required, even for free openings, so we can hold your place. Plan for about an hour.

Admission

September 26 and October 10 are free, with a reservation. Museum admission is $12 for adults, $9 for seniors, $6 for youth from six to seventeen, and free for children five and under. We offer discounts for school groups and for anyone with an EBT card (an electronic benefits card issued by the state of California for CalFresh and other assistance programs). If cost is a concern for you or your family, please write to us at info@paloaltomuseum.org and we will make it work.

With kids

The exhibits are built to include children. Any age can add a family member's photograph to the I Am Palo Alto wall. The Juana Briones room has physical elements children can handle. More hands-on galleries open in the months ahead, including one built around toys and inventions in January.

Accessibility

The building has an elevator and accessible restrooms. Because the museum is opening in stages, we are still confirming which routes and facilities are open on September 26. Write to us at info@paloaltomuseum.org before you come and we will tell you exactly what to expect on the day.

Getting here on foot, by bike, by train, or by bus

The Bryant Street Bike Boulevard runs past our front door, and bike racks are at the entrance. Caltrain to Palo Alto Station leaves you about a ten-minute walk through downtown. VTA bus 21 stops at Waverley Street and Homer Avenue, one block east.

Parking

Free two-hour street parking is available on the surrounding blocks. The Civic Center Garage, under City Hall at 250 Hamilton Avenue, is free for the first three hours and $20 for the day. The Oak Court garage at 260 Homer Avenue is free from 6 to 10 p.m. If you can, please carpool.

About

Nothing here just happened.

In 1844, Juana Briones bought a rancho in her own name and ran it after leaving her husband, when a woman was not supposed to be able to do either. In 1878, Eadweard Muybridge lined up twelve cameras on the Stanford estate and photographed a galloping horse frame by frame. In 1932, doctors on Homer Avenue started a group practice when organized medicine was against it, in the building that is now this museum. In the 1950s, Joseph Eichler sold houses to Black families when the national association of home builders said not to. In 2020, after decades of keeping them out, the town opened Foothills Park to people who do not live here.

Each of these involved arguments, experiments, luck, and choices that could have gone another way. The Palo Alto Museum reconstructs those moments, before the outcome was known, and asks the same question of each: how did this happen?

Why this museum, and why now

Palo Alto's story is preserved in many places already. The historical association, the city archive at Guy Miller, the university library, family papers, private collections. What has been missing is a place that sets different kinds of those stories next to one another and looks at them the same way.

How the museum works

Every exhibition is joined by talks, performances, classes, and hands-on activities planned alongside it. A visit to the museum is not the whole experience. Coming back is.

We also ask questions. Each opening puts one question to the community about what we build next. You are not visiting a finished museum. You have a voice in what comes next.

What community input has already changed

Two exhibits opening this fall were shaped directly by what people told us. Several hundred names were considered for the first group of people featured in I Am Palo Alto. Five names came up again and again, across three rounds of community input, and all five are in the exhibit. And Juana Briones' room was expanded, on community request, to place her life inside the political and social context of her era.

The building and the archive

The building at 300 Homer Avenue was built in 1932 for the Palo Alto Medical Clinic. Its outside wall carries seventeen frescoes painted by Victor Arnautoff, who had studied fresco technique under Diego Rivera. In the X-ray panel, the patient is Arnautoff himself. One of the clinic's founding doctors thought another of the panels should be painted over. The argument stopped traffic on Homer Avenue for a time. The frescoes are still there.

The town's own record, the Guy Miller Archives, is one of the richer city archives on the peninsula. It is owned and run by the City of Palo Alto and lives inside our building. Alongside it, we are building the museum's own collection of objects, papers, and photographs donated by people who have shaped this town.

Who runs the museum

Marguerite Gong Hancock is the museum's inaugural president and CEO. She works closely with the board of directors and leads the museum's staff, alongside a large group of advisors and volunteers. A gift from the Thoits family made the museum's founding leadership possible.

Where the building work stands

A note on where the building work stands.

Marguerite Gong Hancock, President and CEO  ·  Updated August 2026

Dear friends,

We open on September 26, and then in stages through the spring. Here is where the building work stands, and why we are opening this way.

In January of this year, heavy rain brought water into the basement of our 1932 building. What we thought at first was a single leak turned out, over the following four months, to be a series of them, on different walls and in different locations. We now understand that the problem is broader than the leaks themselves. To protect a nearly hundred-year-old building for the years ahead, we need to waterproof the below-ground walls, improve how water drains from the building and from Heritage Park around it, and control humidity inside so that historic materials and any artifacts on the premises stay safe.

Since the first leak, we have been working on it every day. We are working closely with the City of Palo Alto, which owns the building, and with civil engineers, water specialists and other outside experts. The work is complex and it will take longer than we first hoped.

Rather than keep the whole building closed while this continues, we decided to open in stages. On September 26, the lobby, the Juana Briones exhibit, I Am Palo Alto, and the courtyard open to the public. Between September and April, we will open more of the building as each stage of the water work clears. In April, the upper galleries open and the museum begins its regular schedule.

I will update this page every month with where things stand. When we confirm the April opening date, you will hear it here first.

Thank you for your patience, and for believing in this museum.

Marguerite
